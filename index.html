Tokyo  ·  June 12 – 16

A city that never quite lets you catch up

We landed at Narita on a Thursday morning, jet-lagged and wide-eyed, and Tokyo immediately started pulling us in every direction. The Andaz Toranomon Hills became our anchor — a perch above the city's west side that made even waking up at 3am feel intentional.

Five days isn't enough. It's barely a down payment. But we got Tsukiji, Shibuya, Daikanyama, teamLab Planets, a rooftop anniversary dinner, and the peculiar joy of a convenience store at midnight that somehow had everything we needed.

The rooftop, the anniversary, the city below

One night we had dinner on the Andaz rooftop while Tokyo spread out in every direction — office towers blinking amber, the expressway threading between buildings, the faint outline of Fuji if you knew where to look. It felt like a city that had stopped waiting for permission to be extraordinary.

Tsukiji the next morning was the corrective: chaotic, fishy, gloriously unglamorous. We ate tuna on plastic stools at 8am and it was the best thing any of us had tasted in months.

Hakone  ·  June 17

Steam, silence, and a mountain that appeared without warning

The ryokan was the whole point of Hakone, and it delivered. We arrived via the Hakone-Yumoto switchback railway — the kids decided it was the most fun train they'd ever been on — and checked in to tatami rooms and a kaiseki dinner that went on for what felt like a happy eternity.

The ropeway over Owakudani gave us sulphur clouds and, briefly, Fuji through a gap in the overcast. The aquarium near Lake Ashi was unexpected and worth it. We fell asleep to the sound of the onsen.

Dinner as ceremony

The kaiseki at the ryokan arrived in eleven courses, each on its own lacquerware. Quinn counted. Shea ate everything, including the things she said she wouldn't. Riley photographed each plate before anyone could touch it. Nobody complained about any of it.

The next morning we soaked in the outdoor onsen while the mist sat low on the valley and someone's phone was mercifully elsewhere.

Kyoto  ·  June 18 – 21

The city that requires nothing from you except attention

We stayed in a machiya — a traditional townhouse in the older part of the city — and it reset everything. After the density of Tokyo, Kyoto's particular quietness was almost disorienting. Streets wide enough for one bicycle. Gardens that had been tended for three hundred years. Temple bells at six in the morning.

Kinkaku-ji before the crowds. Fushimi Inari twice — once as a family at dawn, and once later just with the kids when the gates went amber in the evening light. Arashiyama on a rickshaw that the kids will talk about for years.

Fushimi Inari, twice

The first time we went as a family, early, before the tour groups. The torii gates ran up the mountain in an unbroken tunnel of vermilion, and the only sounds were our footsteps and the occasional crow. It was one of those places that exceeds the photograph you've already seen a hundred times.

The second visit, just the four of us kids, was different — looser, more exploratory. We made it further up the mountain than the first time. Riley found a shrine off the main path that nobody else seemed to be visiting.

Nara  ·  June 21

A deer walked up and took the cracker right out of Quinn's hand

We came to Nara for the afternoon and it was exactly the right amount. The deer are not shy. They are also not particularly dignified, but they are magnificent, and the sight of them wandering between the temple buildings — treating Tōdai-ji like a very large grazing field — is one of those things that stops feeling real after a while.

We rode rickshaws here too, through the old streets near Kasuga-taisha. By this point in the trip, the kids had stopped asking what was next and started just looking at what was there.

Coming back to Tokyo

We returned to Tokyo for the last night — the Park Hyatt in Shinjuku, which made everyone feel slightly sophisticated after ten days of learning how small a tatami room can be. We walked to Asakusa in the evening, our last temple, and ate ramen at a counter restaurant where you ordered by vending machine and nobody spoke.

The flight home was quiet. Japan does that — it gives you enough that you spend the whole journey back just turning it over, looking at it from different angles, wondering what you missed.
